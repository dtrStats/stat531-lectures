Two truths and a lie

An AI told me three things about R, very confidently.

  1. R starts counting at 1, not 0.
  2. mean(c(1, 2, NA)) returns 1.5.
  3. c(1, "a", TRUE) turns everything into text.

. . .

Vote with fingers (1, 2, 3): which one is the lie?

The lie was #2

c(10, 20, 30)[1]
#> [1] 10
mean(c(1, 2, NA))
#> [1] NA
c(1, "a", TRUE)
#> [1] "1"    "a"    "TRUE"

. . .

Bug: A confident wrong answer looks exactly like a confident right one.

Who’s driving?

Time 0:08–0:20 · Framing + first live bug.

The new division of labor

AI drives: writes code fast

You navigate: choose the destination, read the map, check the route

. . .

Fast driving is great. Driving into a lake is not.

What does a navigator do?

  1. Set the destination: say clearly what you want
  2. Read the route: understand the code
  3. Check the road: run it, test it
  4. Make the call: accept, fix, or reject

Why learn R if AI writes it?

You can’t catch a wrong turn on a map you can’t read.

. . .

The scariest bugs don’t crash. They run fine and give a believable answer.

The Audit Loop

  1. Read it
  2. Run it
  3. Restate it in plain English
  4. Refine it or Reject it

. . .

Restate is the superpower. If you can’t say what a line does, you can’t vouch for it.

Bug Bounty rules

  • Every bug you catch: +1 point for your team
  • Explain why an AI would make that mistake: +1 bonus
  • Bugs hide in code and in answers that look fine

Keep the tally visible. Call on a different team each round.

Does this answer the request? Hands up: yes / no

No. arrange() sorts ascending by default, so these are the five WORST rated (Disaster Movie 1.9, Epic Movie 2.3, Battlefield Earth 2.4, Viva Knievel! 2.7, Doogal 2.8). No error, no warning. Fix later with desc(). Award points.

Why this one is sneaky

  • No error
  • No warning
  • A clean table that looks right

Data wrangling is a sentence

“Take movies, then keep the dramas, then sort by rating, then show the top 3.”

The pipe |> reads as then.

Same thing, two ways

head(arrange(filter(movies, genre == "Drama"), imdb_rating), 3)
movies |>
  filter(genre == "Drama") |>
  arrange(imdb_rating) |>
  head(3)

You’ll see two pipes

|> is built into R.

%>% is the older tidyverse pipe. AI and old tutorials use both.

They behave the same for what we do.

In Positron the pipe shortcut is Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+M. A setting decides which pipe it inserts.

Bug Court is in session

Each case: the request, the AI’s code, and your verdict.

Guilty = it’s wrong. Innocent = it’s fine.

Case #1: the request

“Keep the Drama and Comedy movies.”

movies |>
  filter(genre == c("Drama", "Comedy"))

Restate it. Guilty or innocent?

Case #1: the evidence

movies |> filter(genre == c("Drama", "Comedy")) |> nrow()
#> [1] 191
movies |> filter(genre %in% c("Drama", "Comedy")) |> nrow()
#> [1] 392

. . .

Bug: == lines the two names up with rows one by one, back and forth. In your own console R also prints a warning. Warnings are clues.

Guilty. 191 vs 392 rows. The recycling makes row 1 compare to “Drama”, row 2 to “Comedy”, row 3 to “Drama”, and so on. Tie back to Challenge 1.

Case #2: the request

“Keep movies that have a DVD release year.”

movies |>
  filter(dvd_rel_year != NA)

Guilty or innocent?

Case #2: the evidence

movies |> filter(dvd_rel_year != NA) |> nrow()
#> [1] 0
movies |> filter(!is.na(dvd_rel_year)) |> nrow()
#> [1] 643

. . .

Bug: Anything compared with NA is NA, and filter() drops those. Zero rows, no error.

Case #2b: where did it go?

nrow(movies)
#> [1] 651
movies |> filter(runtime > 0) |> nrow()
#> [1] 650

. . .

Bug: Every movie has a runtime above 0, yet one vanished: its runtime is NA. filter() quietly drops rows where the condition is NA.

650 vs 651. This is the first appearance of “who disappears silently?” which becomes the cleaning impact audit in Lab 2.

Case #3: the request

“How many documentaries are in the data?”

movies |>
  filter(genre == "Documentary") |>
  nrow()

Guilty or innocent? Look closely at the column names first.

Case #3: the evidence

movies |> filter(genre == "Documentary") |> nrow()
#> [1] 52
movies |> filter(title_type == "Documentary") |> nrow()
#> [1] 55

. . .

Bug: The code is fine. The question was ambiguous. Two columns, two answers (52 vs 55).

Neither answer is wrong. The navigator’s job is to specify which column, or ask the AI which one it used. Great Week-3 theme: a good spec removes ambiguity.

  • filter() keeps rows

Round 1 (by hand)

Among R-rated Feature Films longer than 150 minutes, which has the highest IMDb rating?

Columns: mpaa_rating, title_type, runtime, imdb_rating

Answer: The Godfather, Part II (202 min, IMDb 9.0). Same six rows as Challenge 3: all six are Feature Films, so adding the title_type condition doesn’t change the result. Good moment to point out that the extra condition is still the safer spec.

Round 2 (by hand)

Which Documentary (title_type) has the lowest audience_score?

Answer: Sea Monsters: A Prehistoric Adventure (68). Next are Blind Spot: Hitler’s Secretary (70) and Kicking It (71). Watch for teams that use genre == "Documentary" instead: the question names title_type.

Round 3 (with your AI)

  1. Paste the spec into your AI

movies |>
  group_by(studio) |>
